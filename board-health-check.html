What makes a board healthy?

Not big names. A healthy board is made of members who know their role, show up prepared, give and help raise money, keep an eye on the money, and hold themselves accountable. The size of your board matters far less than whether it actually does those things.

A board in name only is worse than a small board that shows up.

This checks the eight signs that separate a working board from a board on paper. Answer honestly and you'll get a clear health score, your strengths, and the exact things to strengthen first.

1

Check 1 · Clear roles

Does everyone understand they govern, not manage?

A board sets direction and watches over the organization; staff runs the day-to-day. When members dive into operations, meetings stall and staff get micromanaged.

Strong looks like: members who ask "is this the right strategy?" — not "why did we buy that printer?"

2

Check 2 · Engaged & prepared

Do members show up, prepared, and actually participate?

A board that reads the packet and speaks up makes better decisions. Chronic absence or silence is one of the clearest warning signs of a board in decline.

Strong looks like: good attendance, materials read in advance, and most voices in the room.

3

Check 3 · 100% giving

Does every board member make a personal gift?

Funders and major donors expect 100% board giving — it signals everyone has skin in the game. The amount matters far less than the participation.

Strong looks like: every member gives a personally meaningful gift each year, no exceptions.

4

Check 4 · Help raising money

Do members help raise money, not just give it?

Giving is the floor; opening doors, making introductions, and thanking donors is the role. Boards that only "give" leave the hardest work to staff.

Strong looks like: each member doing one real fundraising task — an intro, a thank-you call, a table at the event. (Our Make Your Ask tool helps.)

5

Check 5 · Financial & ethical oversight

Does the board watch the money and the ethics?

Duty of care means watching the finances; duty of loyalty means managing conflicts of interest. A board that can't read its own financials — or has no conflict-of-interest policy — is exposed. These are legal duties, not nice-to-haves.

Strong looks like: financials reviewed at every meeting, and a conflict-of-interest policy everyone signs.

6

Check 6 · Built on purpose

Do you recruit for the skills and connections you're missing?

Great boards are built, not filled. Recruiting only friends-of-friends leaves gaps in skills, networks, and perspective — and those gaps cost you for years.

Strong looks like: a map of the skills and connections you need, and a real pipeline to recruit toward them.

7

Check 7 · Onboarding & growth

Do new members get oriented, and does the board keep learning?

A member who's never properly oriented stays lost for a year. A little ongoing board education keeps everyone sharp and engaged.

Strong looks like: a real onboarding for every new member, plus a bit of board development built into the year. (See Welcome a New Board Member.)

8

Check 8 · Accountability

Does the board evaluate itself — and the executive director?

The two most-avoided board jobs — assessing its own performance and evaluating the ED — are among the most important for the organization's health. Skipping them lets problems quietly grow.

Strong looks like: a simple annual board self-assessment, and a structured, fair ED evaluation.

The 8 marks of a healthy board

Whatever you scored, this is the bar — and a checklist to work toward:

  1. Members who govern, not manage.
  2. Engaged, prepared, present.
  3. 100% personal giving.
  4. Members who help raise money, not just give.
  5. Financial oversight and a conflict-of-interest policy.
  6. Built on purpose for the skills you need.
  7. Real onboarding and ongoing development.
  8. Self-evaluation and a real ED evaluation.
