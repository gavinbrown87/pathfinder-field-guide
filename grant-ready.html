Should you even apply for a grant?

Grants look like free money, so small nonprofits chase them first. But they're competitive and slow — and most rejections aren't about bad writing. They're about bad fit, or an organization that wasn't ready.

A grant you weren't ready for isn't free money. It's 40 hours you'll never get back.

This check looks at the eight things foundation, corporate, and community funders actually look for — where most small nonprofits play. Answer honestly and you'll get a clear readiness score, your strengths, and the exact gaps to fix before you write a word.

1

Check 1 · Tax-exempt status

Are you a registered 501(c)(3) in good standing?

Almost every funder requires proof of tax-exempt status before they'll even read your proposal. No status, no grant — full stop.

Strong looks like: a current 501(c)(3) determination letter, or a fiscal sponsor who has one.

2

Check 2 · A specific need

Can you state the problem with real, specific data?

Funders fund problems they can see. "We help the community" loses. "120 kids in our county are on a tutoring waitlist" wins.

Strong looks like: a need statement backed by a local number. (Our Make Your Case tool helps here.)

3

Check 3 · Measurable outcomes

Do you track results and know what success looks like in numbers?

Funders buy outcomes, not activities. They want to know what changes because of your work — and how you'll measure it.

Strong looks like: "84% of kids gained a reading level," not "we served many children."

4

Check 4 · A real budget

Do you have an annual budget and a budget for the project?

A proposal lives or dies on a credible budget. Funders want to see you've costed the work, not guessed at it.

Strong looks like: a board-approved annual budget plus a line-item budget for the specific project.

5

Check 5 · Financial records

Are your financials in order?

Funders are trusting you with money, so they check whether you handle it well. Messy or missing records end an application fast.

Strong looks like: current financial statements, and a filed Form 990 if your size requires one.

6

Check 6 · Capacity to deliver

Can you run the program AND handle the reporting?

Funders worry whether you can actually deliver — and report back. A grant you can't report on can cost you the next one.

Strong looks like: a named person who owns delivery, and a plan for who handles grant reporting.

7

Check 7 · Funder fit

Have you found funders who actually fund your work?

Most rejections are bad fit, not bad writing. A funder who doesn't fund your cause, your area, or your size will never say yes — no matter how good the proposal.

Strong looks like: a short list of 3–5 funders who fund your cause, in your area, at your grant size.

8

Check 8 · Evidence it works

Can you show your program actually delivers?

New programs can still get funded, but proof lowers a funder's risk — and risk is what they're weighing.

Strong looks like: a result you can point to and one real story of someone it changed.

The 8 marks of a grant-ready nonprofit

Whatever you scored, this is the bar — and a checklist to work toward:

  1. Tax-exempt status (or a fiscal sponsor).
  2. A specific, data-backed need.
  3. Outcomes you measure in numbers.
  4. An annual budget and a project budget.
  5. Financial records in order.
  6. Capacity to deliver and to report.
  7. Funders who actually fit your work.
  8. Evidence — results and a story — that it works.
