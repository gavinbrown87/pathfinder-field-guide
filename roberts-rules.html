The Pathfinder Field Guide · Guide

Robert's Rules, simplified.

The official manual runs about 700 pages. Your board needs about one. Here's how to actually make decisions in a meeting — without the intimidation.

The short version

To decide something: someone moves it, someone seconds it, the chair restates it, the board discusses, then votes, and the chair announces the result. That's 90% of what you'll ever use.

The one loop that runs every decision

Robert's Rules exists so a group can decide things in an orderly way — letting the majority rule while every voice gets heard. Strip away the detail and almost every decision follows the same six steps:

  1. Move it. A member says, "I move that we…" — proposing a specific action.
  2. Second it. Another member says, "I second." (No second? The motion dies.)
  3. Restate it. The chair repeats the motion so everyone's clear on what's being decided.
  4. Discuss it. The board debates. The chair keeps order — one person at a time.
  5. Vote. The chair calls for "ayes," then "noes." Most motions pass by a simple majority.
  6. Announce it. The chair states the result and any next step.
The shortcut every chair should know: when the board clearly agrees, skip the formal vote. The chair says, "If there's no objection, we'll adopt the motion to…" — pauses — and if no one objects, "Hearing none, it's adopted." Fast, and fully legitimate.

The handful of motions you'll actually use

Main motionProposes a new action or decision. This is the one you'll use most.
AmendChanges the wording of the motion on the floor. "I move to amend the motion to…" — also needs a second and a vote.
PostponeDelays the decision to a later time or meeting, when you need more information.
TableSets a motion aside — often to kill it, at least for now.
Call the questionEnds debate and forces a vote. Because it cuts off discussion, it takes a two-thirds vote to pass.
AdjournEnds the meeting. "I move to adjourn" — second, quick vote, done.

When you need to interrupt: "points"

Some things need attention right away and don't require a motion or a vote. Any member can simply state one:

  • Point of order — "We're not following the rules / the agenda." Flags a procedure problem.
  • Point of information — "I need a fact to vote well." A genuine question, not a speech in disguise.
  • Point of personal privilege — "I can't hear," or "It's too hot in here." Addresses the conditions in the room.

A few notes for the chair

  • Follow the agenda — it's what keeps the meeting moving toward its goals.
  • Recognize people before they speak, and let everyone speak once before anyone speaks twice.
  • When discussion wanders, gently steer it back. Park off-topic ideas for later.
  • Model courtesy, and insist on it. Let the group do its work — don't over-command.

You don't need to master 700 pages. You need a loop, a shortcut, and the nerve to keep things moving.
